Engineering Ethics (02234213): Midterm Preparation Suggestions


Eastern University, School of Engineering | Midterm
Examination, Fall 2026



The midterm rewards students who can argue a position, not
students who can recite definitions. It carries 30 marks and runs 1.5 hours.
Section A (Questions 1 and 2) is compulsory. In Section B you answer either
Question 3 or Question 4. Every question has three parts: part (a) is worth 2
marks and asks you to explain a concept, part (b) is worth 3 marks and asks you
to analyze, and part (c) is worth 5 marks and asks you to evaluate. Half of
your marks therefore sit in the (c) parts, so spend your revision time on
building arguments, not on memorizing lists.


How to write each type of part



For part (a), give a tight, accurate answer in four to six
sentences. Define the term, name the thinker if there is one, and add one
sentence that shows why it matters in engineering. Two marks do not need a
page.



For part (b), take the concept apart and then say how its
pieces behave in a real workplace. A bare description of a model earns little.
Show how each component works, then show where it breaks down for an engineer
under pressure. One short example is enough.



For part (c), state your position in the first sentence,
then defend it. Give the strongest reason for your view, answer the strongest
objection against it, and finish with a conclusion that follows from what you
wrote. Use a named case as evidence. A one-sided answer, however well written,
will lose marks.



The verb in the question tells you what to do. Differentiate
and compare mean you set two things side by side and show where they differ.
Deconstruct and analyze mean you break the idea into parts. Evaluate, assess,
appraise and justify mean you reach a judgment and give reasons for it.


Time plan



You have about three minutes per mark. Give roughly 4
minutes to each (a), 7 minutes to each (b) and 12 minutes to each (c). That
puts Section A at about 70 minutes and leaves about 20 minutes for your Section
B question, with a few minutes spare to read your answers. Do not overspend on
part (a) of any question.


Question 1 themes: normative ethics, moral agency and whistleblowing


Part (a). Revise the difference between descriptive ethics,
which reports what people actually believe and do, and normative ethics, which
says what people ought to do. Be ready to explain why a professional code of
conduct is normative: it prescribes duties and does not describe habits. Use
one clause from an engineering code to make the point.



Part (b). Learn James Rest's four components in order: moral
sensitivity (noticing that an issue is ethical), moral judgment (deciding what
is right), moral motivation (giving ethics priority over other goals) and moral
character or implementation (having the courage and skill to act). The analysis
question lives in the gap between the second and fourth components. An engineer
can reason well and still stay silent because a manager has set a deadline, a
promotion is at stake, or colleagues are all saying nothing. Practise
explaining that gap in your own words.



Part (c). Prepare both sides. Confidentiality matters
because employers and clients trust engineers with sensitive information. The
public interest comes first because the codes of most engineering bodies place
safety, health and welfare of the public above loyalty to an employer. Then
learn a standard set of conditions for justified whistleblowing, such as
Richard De George's: the harm is serious, the engineer has reported it
internally, internal channels have been exhausted, the evidence is documented
and would convince a reasonable observer, and there is a reasonable chance that
going public will prevent the harm. Be ready to say when the duty shifts from
permitted to obligatory. Rana Plaza (2013) and the Challenger launch decision
(1986) both work as cases, because engineers or inspectors raised warnings that
were overridden.


Question 2 themes: Mill, organizational failure and cost-benefit analysis



Part (a). Know Mill's claim that pleasures differ in quality
and not only in quantity, and the test he proposed: those who have experienced
both kinds prefer the higher one. Link this to the familiar criticism that
utilitarianism reduces life to a pleasure counter, and explain how the quality
distinction answers that charge. The phrase about being a dissatisfied Socrates
rather than a satisfied fool is worth knowing, but explain it in your own
words.



Part (b). Normalization of deviance is Diane Vaughan's term
from her study of Challenger. A team accepts a small departure from a safety
standard, nothing goes wrong, and the departure becomes the new normal.
Diffusion of responsibility is the psychological tendency to feel less
personally accountable when many people share a task. Your analysis should show
how each one dulls moral sensitivity: people stop seeing a risk as a risk, or
assume someone else will deal with it.



Part (c). Learn why cost-benefit analysis is attractive: it
is transparent, it allows comparison, and it forces decision makers to count
consequences. Then learn its limits. Putting a price on a life is contested,
the people who bear the risk are often not the people who gain the benefit, and
averages can hide serious harm to a few. The Ford Pinto fuel tank case is the
standard example. Your argument for non-consequentialist constraints should
draw on rights, informed consent and respect for persons, and you should show
how these could be written into a risk protocol as limits that no cost-benefit
result can override.


Question 3 themes: divine command, care ethics and Kant



Part (a). Divine Command Theory holds that an act is right
because God commands it. The Euthyphro Dilemma asks whether God commands an act
because it is already right, which would mean rightness exists independently of
God, or whether it is right only because God commands it, which would make
morality arbitrary. State both horns clearly and say why each troubles the
theory.



Part (b). Kohlberg's model emphasizes justice, rights and
impartial principles. Gilligan responded that many people reason from
relationships, responsibility and care for particular others. Compare the two,
then show what care ethics adds to engineering design: attention to vulnerable
users, long-term consequences for communities, and the human side of a
technical decision. Pick a design example, such as a building or a product used
by children or elderly people, and use it.



Part (c). Know the Formula of Humanity: treat humanity, in
yourself and in others, always as an end and never merely as a means. Link it
to engineering through safety standards, informed consent and privacy. An
engineer who hides a known defect, or who collects user data without their
knowledge, is using people as mere means. Evaluate honestly. Kant gives a
strong basis for duties to users, but the formula can be hard to apply when
duties conflict, and it says little about weighing risks across a whole population.
A balanced answer notes this.


Question 4 themes: value theory, stress and double effect



Part (a). Intrinsic value is value as an end in itself;
instrumental value is value as a means to something else. For the second half,
think of design standards and codes: a standard for fire exits or load limits
is not neutral, because it expresses a decision about how much risk society
will accept and whose safety counts. Prepare one concrete example.



Part (b). Cover how heavy workloads, long hours and tight
deadlines wear down an engineer's attention and willingness to speak up, and
how prolonged stress can lower commitment to the organization. Distinguish the
effect on moral motivation from the effect on organizational commitment, and
say which direction each moves. Keep the answer tied to engineering work and
not to stress in general.



Part (c). Learn the Doctrine of Double Effect and its four
conditions: the act itself is good or at least neutral, the good effect is not
produced by means of the bad effect, the agent intends only the good effect,
and there is a proportionately serious reason for allowing the bad effect.
Apply them to a design trade-off, such as a vehicle or bridge design that
cannot remove every risk. Then evaluate: the doctrine gives a structure for
thinking about unavoidable risk, but critics say intention is hard to verify
and that the proportionality test needs the very cost-benefit judgments the
doctrine was meant to limit.

Choosing in Section B



Question 3 leans on moral theory, so it suits you if you are
comfortable with philosophical arguments. Question 4 leans on applied
reasoning, so it suits you if you prefer working through a design scenario.
Revise both, and decide in the exam room after reading each question once.


Practice


For each question, write one part (c) answer under timed
conditions, about 12 minutes, and then check whether your first sentence states
a position and whether you answered the best objection. Build a small bank of
cases you can use in any answer: Challenger, Ford Pinto, Rana Plaza, and one
more of your own choosing. For each, be able to say in two sentences what
happened and which ethical idea it illustrates. Study with a partner and mark
each other's answers using the verb in the question as the standard.
